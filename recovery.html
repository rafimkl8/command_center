Start here — your first hour

The planning is finished. Day 1, one hour, no decisions to make:

0:00Blank page, 10 minutes. Write from memory, nothing open: what is scope? What does a function return? What is the difference between a parameter and an argument? What do *args and **kwargs do? It will be short and it will be uncomfortable. That is the measurement, not the failure.
0:10Learn, 30 minutes, one source only. Functions and scope. Stop at 30 minutes even if you are mid-sentence. One source — not four tabs.
0:40Empty file, 20 minutes, no reference. Write five functions: one with a default parameter, one returning two values, one taking *args, one taking **kwargs, one that mutates a list passed into it — and notice what that does.
1:00Close the laptop. Done for the day on backfill.

The goal of hour one is not to learn functions. It is to prove the loop works — that you can go from not knowing to having written something yourself, in sixty minutes, without AI. Once you have done that once, you have a repeatable unit and the remaining two months are just that unit pointed at different topics.

The daily loop — the only thing you have to maintain

Do not try to maintain a two-month plan. Maintain one shape of day, and let the plan tell you which topic to point it at. The topic changes constantly. The loop never changes.

10 minBlank page from memory, before anything opens.
40 minLearn, timeboxed, one source. AI may explain and quiz. AI may not author.
40 minRebuild from empty. The step that does the actual work. Never skip it.
10 minExplain it in one paragraph, in English. This is your English track — not extra work.
2 minTick it in the audit. Log anything AI wrote as a 1.

Roughly 100 minutes of real work. Everything else in a study day is support.

Define your floor now, before you need it. On the worst day — hospital, family crisis, no sleep — the minimum is one 20-minute loop: 5 minutes blank page, 10 learning, 5 rebuilding. That is a complete day. It counts.

You had no floor in module 8, so the floor became zero, and zero days are what compound into eleven-module gaps. A 20-minute day is not a broken streak. It is the streak.

Daily shape — and the part that is not negotiable

Before the break (Aug 6–15): about 4 focused hours a day. During the break: 6 focused hours, 6 days a week, one full day off. Not 12 hours. You are rebuilding from a fragile place, and a plan that burns you out in week one is worse than no plan at all.

  • Morning block (post-Fajr, ~3h) — hardest new concept of the day. This is your best brain; do not spend it on video.
  • Afternoon block (~2h) — reconstruct from empty. Build the small thing. No tutorials.

The Friday checkpoint — 30 minutes, non-negotiable

Modules 8–18 did not fail because the plan was wrong. They failed because nothing ever forced the drift to become visible. By the time you felt it, it was three months deep. This is the fix, and it is thirty minutes a week:

  1. Open the audit. Tick what you cleared. Log anything AI wrote as a 1 — that is the whole safety mechanism.
  2. Look at the deadline chips on each track. Am I on pace for the next one, honestly?
  3. If behind: pick one thing to cut, using the sacrifice order below. Cut it deliberately, write it down, do not just hope.
  4. Three sentences: what I learned, what is still fuzzy, what I am doing next week.

Thirty minutes. That is the entire governance layer. Skip it for two weeks in a row and you are back in the situation that put you here — not because you stopped working, but because you stopped being able to see.

Track three numbers. Ignore the rest.

  • Did I do a loop today? Yes or no. Even the 20-minute floor counts as yes.
  • Concept debt count. One number, from the audit. It should fall every single week.
  • Did I submit my own work? From Aug 16 onward this is yes or no, and nothing else.

LeetCode totals, commit streaks, article counts, applications sent — all real, all later. Tracking them now just gives you more ways to feel behind.

Early warning signs — catch the slide at week one

These are the specific behaviours that preceded the last collapse. Two of them in one week is a signal, not a character flaw:

  • You skip the blank page because "I already know this one." You do not — that is what the blank page is for.
  • You skip the rebuild-from-empty because time is short. This is the dangerous one. It is the only step that actually builds anything, and cutting it does not feel like cutting anything.
  • You watch a class recording instead of building something. Comfort disguised as diligence.
  • You open AI before the 25 minutes are up.
  • You reorganise the tracker instead of studying. Tidying the system feels like progress and produces nothing. Watch for this one especially — the system is finished now.

The response to a warning sign is never to quit the day. It is to shrink the day to the floor and keep the chain intact.

When life happens: what to cut, in order

You are now scheduled continuously from Aug 6 to Oct 3, and your wife's family situation is still unresolved. Something will go wrong, because something always does. So decide the sacrifice order now, while you are calm, instead of at midnight while you are not:

  1. Cut first: CSS depth. Learn it on demand and accept ugly.
  2. Then: LeetCode and DSA. Already parked; keep them parked longer.
  3. Then: project polish — READMEs, deployment, styling. Working and ugly beats unfinished and pretty.
  4. Then: module project scope. Build three honest pages instead of ten.
  5. Last resort: slip the JavaScript deadline into module 26 and take one rough React module.

Never cut these, in any circumstance: sleep, the evenings that belong to your wife, or the reconstruct-from-empty step. The first two break you. The third quietly turns studying back into watching, which is the exact failure that cost you modules 8–18 — and it is the one that hides, because it still feels like work.

How long do you keep using AI? Until Aug 16.

A real answer, not a comforting one: module 20 is the last assignment you lean on AI for. From module 21 onward — Aug 16 — you write what you submit.

That is credible because of the sequence, not because of willpower. Aug 6–15 gives you Python's class system and a Django CRUD app you built yourself. A cart and a product list are the same skills with one more relationship. By Aug 16 you will not need the crutch for the thing in front of you.

For module 20 this week, use AI like this instead of the way you have been:

  • Read the assignment and write, in plain words, what you think the solution needs. Ten minutes, before AI sees it.
  • Let AI produce it.
  • Then spend twenty minutes making it yours: for every line you do not understand, ask "explain this line, why is it needed, what breaks without it?" — never "write me the next part".
  • Break it on purpose. Delete a line, read the error, put it back yourself. Errors are the cheapest teacher you have.
  • Log every concept you could not have written as a 1 in the audit, in the same sitting.
  • Three sentences in the README: what it does, why it is built this way, what you still do not understand.

That turns a submission worth nothing into one worth maybe forty percent — and, more importantly, it leaves a visible debt instead of a silent one.

Do not expect module 21 to be pretty. Attend all six classes; the exposure is worth real money even when you follow half of it. Then build the smallest honest slice yourself. An ugly three-page shop you can explain line by line beats a complete storefront you cannot — the first is a portfolio piece and an interview story, the second is a liability any competent interviewer strips in twenty minutes.

Why this makes you an engineer, not a coder

Writing code from a clear spec is the part AI genuinely took. That was the old entry-level job and it is not coming back. What went up in value is everything around it: deciding what to build, decomposing a vague problem, spotting the subtly wrong answer, knowing what breaks at 10x load.

You cannot review what you cannot read. That is the whole argument. The person who can only prompt is the person who gets replaced; the person who can verify is the one who gets paid. Your instinct to learn this properly is not idealism — it is the employability strategy.

So from now on, every project gets three sentences written in its README:

  • Why did I choose this design over the obvious alternative?
  • What breaks first if traffic goes up 10x?
  • What would I do differently with another week?

Three sentences. No extra study hours. That is the judgment muscle that separates the two job titles, and it is also the exact material interviews are made of.
